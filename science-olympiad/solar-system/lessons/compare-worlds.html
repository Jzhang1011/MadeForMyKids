Compare Worlds

⏱ ~15 min · 🎯 You will be able to use the Venus–Earth–Mars contrast to explain how atmospheres, water loss, and magnetic fields decide a planet's fate.

1 Watch ~2 min

Venus, Earth, and Mars are rocky planets that formed in the same solar system. Evidence suggests early Mars had liquid water at its surface — yet today it's a frozen desert. Why did they end up so different?

Watch for: what Earth has that Mars is missing — and what the solar wind does to a planet without it.

3 Understand ~4 min

Venus, Earth, and Mars are rocky neighbors. Evidence suggests early Mars had liquid water at its surface, while scientists continue to investigate how the atmospheres and climates of all three worlds evolved. (You already know Venus's story from Lesson 1; here it's one line, because the comparison is the point.)

🔥

Venus: the oven

Runaway greenhouse effect — thick CO₂ trapped the heat, and any early oceans boiled away. Lesson 1 covered this; it's here for contrast.

🌍

Earth: the survivor

Earth maintains conditions that allow liquid water at its surface. Its distance from the Sun, atmosphere, carbon cycle, and other planetary processes all contribute to its climate. Earth's magnetic field also affects how the solar wind interacts with its surroundings — but no single factor explains habitability on its own.

🧊

Mars: the stripped world

Mars was once warmer and wetter, with evidence that liquid water flowed across its surface. Over billions of years, Mars lost much of its atmosphere through several processes, including interactions with the solar wind and the escape of gases into space — NASA's MAVEN mission measures this escape happening today. Its smaller size and weaker gravity also make it harder to retain an atmosphere. As the atmosphere thinned, liquid water became less stable at the surface.

How Mars lost its water — the chain of events

Common misconception: "Mars is cold just because it's farther from the Sun." Distance contributes — but the thinning of its atmosphere is a bigger part of the story. Lesson 1 already proved distance isn't destiny: Venus is farther than Mercury and far hotter. Same logic applies here.
About magnetic fields: scientists investigate magnetic fields as one factor that can slow atmospheric loss — Mars's story is the evidence. It isn't a universal requirement, and it doesn't decide who keeps an atmosphere: Venus has no global field yet holds 92 bar, because its Earth-like size and gravity hold gas tightly. (This matters for Lesson 3: icy moons can hide oceans with no magnetic shield at all.)

5 Apply ~2 min

Scientist's challenge: which world deserves a closer look? Two rocky exoplanets orbit similar stars.

  • Planet A: has a thick CO₂-rich atmosphere. Its surface temperature and pressure have not yet been measured accurately.
  • Planet B: has a very thin atmosphere and orbits a star with frequent strong stellar activity. Scientists have not determined whether it has subsurface liquid water.

Which conclusion is best supported by the evidence?

6 Review ~1 min

Three key takeaways
  1. Venus, Earth, and Mars started as rocky neighbors — their atmospheres and gravity wrote three different endings.
  2. Mars lost much of its atmosphere through several processes — solar-wind-driven escape and sputtering among them — and its small size and weak gravity made the loss worse. MAVEN measures the escape happening today.
  3. Distance is only a starting point. Compare atmospheres — and check what the evidence actually supports — before predicting a planet's fate.
Misconception check: "Mars is cold because it's far from the Sun." What's missing? Answer: the stripped atmosphere — thin air can't trap heat, and Venus proves distance alone doesn't decide temperature.
