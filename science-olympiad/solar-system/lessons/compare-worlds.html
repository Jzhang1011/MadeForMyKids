Compare Worlds

⏱ ~15 min · 🎯 You will be able to use the Venus–Earth–Mars contrast to explain how atmospheres, water loss, and magnetic fields decide a planet's fate.

1 Watch ~2 min

Mars and Earth started out surprisingly alike — thick air, flowing water. So why is one a blue marble and the other a frozen desert?

Watch for: what Earth has that Mars is missing — and what the solar wind does to a planet without it.

2 Explore ~3 min

🔮 Predict first: which two worlds do you expect to be most alike — and which pair will surprise you most? Pick any two and compare.

Data: NASA planetary fact sheets, reviewed 2026-10-09. Temperatures are day–night averages; pressures are surface values.

🤔 Use the contrast: look at your two worlds. Which single factor explains most of the difference between them — distance, atmosphere, gravity, or magnetic field? The 🔍 contrast line under the comparator is your answer key — score your prediction against it.

3 Understand ~4 min

Three rocky planets, similar starting ingredients — three completely different fates. (You already know Venus's story from Lesson 1; here it's one line, because the comparison is the point.)

🔥

Venus: the oven

Runaway greenhouse effect — thick CO₂ trapped the heat, and any early oceans boiled away. Lesson 1 covered this; it's here for contrast.

🌍

Earth: the survivor

Right distance plus a global magnetic field deflecting the solar wind plus active geology recycling carbon. The atmosphere stayed balanced — so the water stayed liquid.

🧊

Mars: the stripped world

The leading account: about 4 billion years ago Mars lost its global magnetic field, and without that shield the solar wind spent eons stripping the atmosphere into space — NASA's MAVEN spacecraft measures the escape still happening today. Mars is also much smaller than Earth or Venus, so its weaker gravity holds gas less tightly. Thin air can't trap heat or keep water liquid: rivers became ice and dust.

How Mars lost its water — the chain of events

Common misconception: "Mars is cold just because it's farther from the Sun." Distance contributes — but the stripped atmosphere is the bigger story. Lesson 1 already proved distance isn't destiny: Venus is farther than Mercury and far hotter. Same logic applies here.
About magnetic fields: scientists investigate magnetic fields as one factor that can slow atmospheric loss — Mars's story is the evidence. It isn't a universal requirement, and it doesn't decide who keeps an atmosphere: Venus has no global field yet holds 92 bar, because its Earth-like size and gravity hold gas tightly. (This matters for Lesson 3: icy moons can hide oceans with no magnetic shield at all.)

4 Practice ~3 min

Try each question before checking — you'll get an explanation either way.

5 Apply ~2 min

Two candidates, one distance. Two exoplanets orbit at the same distance around similar stars. Planet A has a thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere. Planet B's star is extremely active, with a fierce stellar wind — and B shows almost no atmosphere at all. Which is the better habitability candidate, and what single measurement would most change your mind?

Model answer. Planet A is the better candidate: an atmosphere can trap heat and support liquid water, while B looks like Mars after the stripping — thin air, frozen surface. The measurement that could flip it: water vapor (or surface water) on B anyway — e.g., a subsurface ocean like Europa's, which doesn't need an atmosphere. Or the reverse flip: finding A's atmosphere is a runaway greenhouse like Venus would sink A instead. One measurement, honestly weighed, beats a confident guess.

6 Review ~1 min

Three key takeaways
  1. Venus, Earth, and Mars started alike — their atmospheres wrote three different endings.
  2. Mars lost its global magnetic field ~4 billion years ago; the solar wind then stripped its air (MAVEN measures the escape today).
  3. Distance is only a starting point. Compare atmospheres before predicting a planet's fate.
Misconception check: "Mars is cold because it's far from the Sun." What's missing? Answer: the stripped atmosphere — thin air can't trap heat, and Venus proves distance alone doesn't decide temperature.

Self-check: explain the Mars water-loss chain in your own words — field, wind, air, water.
