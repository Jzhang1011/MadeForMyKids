Visual schedules

One step at a time: how visual schedules help neurodivergent kids stay on track

A visual schedule turns the day into something a child can see, follow, and finish — one step at a time. Here is how to build one, and what it looks like when the routine itself plays out on screen.

13 November 2026 · Family Board

What is a visual schedule?

A visual schedule shows the day as a sequence a child can see: pictures, words, or both, laid out in order. Wake up, brush teeth, breakfast, shoes on, out the door. The child does not have to hold the plan in their head or wait for an adult to announce it. They can look, and know what is next.

For neurodivergent children — autistic children, children with ADHD, children who find transitions hard — that visibility matters more than it does for anyone else. Spoken instructions vanish the moment they are said. A picture stays put.

Why they work

The reason visual schedules keep showing up in classrooms and clinics is not fashion. Visual supports are one of the 28 practices identified as evidence-based for autistic children and young people in the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice review (Hume and colleagues, 2021), backed by 65 studies. A separate review by Knight, Sartini and Spriggs (2014) reached the same conclusion for visual activity schedules specifically: they work, from preschool through adulthood, especially when an adult teaches the child how to use the schedule and then gradually steps back.

What the research keeps finding is consistent: children follow the schedule themselves more often, need fewer adult prompts, and handle the switch from one activity to the next with less distress. The mechanism is not mysterious. A day that can be seen in advance is a day with fewer surprises, and transitions stop feeling like ambushes.

What makes a good one

A schedule on the wall only helps if the child can actually use it. Four things matter most:

  1. Pictures plus words. Pair a simple image with a short label, so the schedule works whether or not the child reads fluently yet.
  2. Same place, every day. Put it where the day happens — the kitchen, the hallway — and keep it there, so checking it becomes a habit rather than a hunt.
  3. Anchors first. Start with the big fixed points of the day (school, dinner, bedtime), then layer the smaller routines in between.
  4. Teach it, then fade. Walk through the schedule together at first: point to each step, do it together, and help a little less each time. The goal is for the schedule, not your voice, to carry the routine. Change one thing at a time, and give warning before a change lands.

What it looks like on Family Board

Family Board is a shared family calendar that lives on a tablet in the kitchen. Its difference is the Step Player: routines do not sit on the calendar as a line to check off — they play, one step at a time, with a photo and a spoken prompt for each step.

Inside a routine, step by step

Some tasks are routines in themselves — brushing teeth, getting dressed, leaving the house. On Family Board those run as step-by-step routines: one step at a time, each with a photo and a spoken prompt, and a Done button to move on. The routine is linked straight from the calendar task, so the child taps the task and the steps begin. If they get stuck, the grown-up is alerted.

This is the part other family calendars do not have. Skylight’s calendar routines are flat checklists, and its step-by-step device, Buddy, is a separate $139.99 purchase per child that is not linked to the calendar task. Here the steps are part of the calendar itself, and any routine can carry the child’s own photos — the toothbrush they actually use, the shoes they actually wear — so the prompt shows their world, not a stock picture.

The day at a glance

Behind the routine sits the day itself. The board shows the whole day in order, colour-coded per child. A child can see what is done, what is now, and what is next without asking. Tapping a finished task crosses it out and earns a star, so progress is visible as it happens.

One thing at a time

For children who find a full day overwhelming, Guided day strips the board down to the present moment: a big clock, the next task, and nothing else competing for attention. The child follows one step, finishes it, and the next one appears.

The grown-ups build it together

Both parents can edit the same calendar from their own phones — sharing is free, and either adult can add, change, or rearrange tasks. The board on the wall updates for everyone at once, so the schedule the child sees is always the current one.

Finishing feels good

Stars add up through the week, and children can trade them for rewards the family chooses together — a movie night, a special outing. The reward is not the point; the point is that finishing is visible, and the child can see their own progress.

A sample morning

Here is a morning built the way the research suggests — anchors first, one thing at a time:

Wake up → brush teeth → get dressed → breakfast → shoes and backpack → out the door.

On Family Board, “brush teeth” is a calendar task with a step-by-step routine attached: pick up the toothbrush, put toothpaste on the brush, brush the top teeth, and so on — each step with its own photo. The child taps the task, follows the steps, and the task crosses out with a star. The rest of the morning is already on the board, in order, waiting.

Want the same routine? The brushing-teeth routine above is shared in the Step Player community library, so any family can copy it and add their own photos. Routines shared to the library carry the step text only — photos and voice stay private to the family that made them.

A note on what this is

A visual schedule is a support for daily life, not a treatment. It does not diagnose, treat, or replace the care of a doctor, psychologist, therapist, or teacher. What it does is make the day predictable and doable — and for many families, that is what changes the morning.
